Why this became an investigation

Correlate evidence. Don’t trust a “suspicious” label.

The finding

R3 links the authoritative effective-removal event to an allowed read by the affected account, within the same application, resource and declared time window.

The source labeled the later read allowed / member. SignalBridge found the conflict by joining the observations.

Case 1576faa9-6deb-4511-804c-81dafa8bb648
Rule R3 · Priority high
Recorded disposition: open

The controls that make it credible

  • The removed member’s session still worked; login failure did not explain the denial.
  • The owner could still read the document; a broken service did not explain the denial.
  • Cross-app accounts and outsiders were denied in both apps.
  • Re-grants and alternate grants preserved legitimate access.
  • The bounded defect was reset, followed by denial and owner checks.

Restoration was observed. The case remained open in this run: no human analyst decision or independent remediation approval is claimed.

Why it matters

Removing access isn't the same as ending it.

The risk

A user can stay signed in after losing a permission. I wanted to show that monitoring can catch a private read that contradicts an authoritative access change.

What this run shows

In this isolated lab, the removed account read a known private record under a deliberate defect. Resetting the defect restored the denial, and the owner's access kept working.

Evidence boundary

  • Recorded synthetic reference lab; not production or live monitoring.
  • One deliberately introduced regression is not a detection-accuracy benchmark.
  • The case remained open; reset checks are not independent analyst approval of remediation.
  • Wazuh inputs were staged in the source run. A later native collector attempt collected zero of 24 inputs and its launcher was retired. No successful enterprise collection is claimed.
